A Threatening Voicemail Reaches the Office. Preserve It Before You Call Back.

A practical self-storage response for preserving a threatening voicemail, screening immediate danger and handing callback and closure decisions to named authority.

By Jared Mastroianni9 min read

The illustration does not depict a real incident or the fictional Red Willow Storage example.

A self-storage manager opens the office and finds a voicemail that includes a threat. The message may mention the manager, another employee, the property or a disputed account. The caller may identify themselves, or the number may be blocked. No one is standing at the counter yet.

The natural response is to call back and settle the problem. That is exactly where a customer-service habit can outrun a safety process.

A threatening voicemail is not an ordinary callback. It is a safety signal, an electronic record and an unresolved event. The manager’s first job is not to decide whether the caller “meant it.” The first job is to protect people, preserve the original message and put the decision in the hands of the people authorized to assess and respond.

The process should be simple enough to use at opening, specific enough to preserve evidence and disciplined enough to avoid turning one message into a dozen uncontrolled copies.

Start with immediate danger, not account history

Before reading the customer ledger or dialing the number, ask what is happening now.

  • Is the caller or another named person visible on the property?
  • Does the message describe an immediate or time-specific action?
  • Is there a weapon, fire, medical emergency or active attempt to enter?
  • Is an employee alone, outside or expected to arrive through the affected route?
  • Does anyone need to move to the facility’s designated safe area?

If there is immediate physical danger or another emergency requiring police, fire or medical assistance, call 911. The National 911 Program says callers should be ready to provide the location, callback number, nature of the emergency and known details. Give the call-taker observed facts and follow the instructions provided. Do not delay an emergency call while trying to finish a transcript, reach a regional manager or prove who owns the phone number.

If there is no apparent immediate danger, that does not make the voicemail routine. It means the event can move through the facility’s non-emergency threat-reporting path without an employee improvising urgency or credibility.

Keep the original message intact

The message itself is the first source. Preserve it before creating summaries.

Do not delete it, trim it, rename it, forward it to a group chat or play it over the front-office speaker. Do not use a personal phone to make an informal recording unless the approved procedure specifically permits that method. Each extra copy creates another custody, privacy and retention problem.

Record the system that received the voicemail, the mailbox, displayed caller information, received time, duration and message identifier if the platform provides one. Note whether the message was already marked heard when discovered. If the system has an approved export or preservation function, use it or route the request to the system owner. Do not experiment with settings on the original record.

The FBI Threat Intimidation Guide advises recipients not to delete electronic threats and to preserve electronic evidence. Its guidance covers several threat channels. A self-storage voicemail procedure still needs to match the facility’s systems, policy and local authority, but the evidence principle is clear: keep the original before working from a copy.

Write exact words separately from interpretation

A manager may need a working transcript so the authorized reviewer does not have to rely on memory. Label it as a transcript or working note, not the original evidence.

Write what can actually be heard. Mark uncertain words as uncertain. Record pauses or background sounds only when they are relevant and audible. Do not improve the grammar, complete unfinished sentences or turn a paraphrase into quotation marks. If a second authorized person reviews the transcript, record that review rather than silently changing the first version.

Keep interpretation in a separate field. “Caller says they will arrive when the office opens” is content. “Caller is definitely coming” is an inference. “Raised voice” may be an observable audio characteristic. “Unstable,” “crazy,” “drunk,” “harmless” and “just venting” are unsupported labels.

That separation matters because a dismissive label can suppress a real concern, while an inflated label can spread fear and accusation. The record should help a qualified person assess the event, not pre-decide it.

Protect people without broadcasting the message

The office may need an operating change before anyone resolves the underlying account dispute. That can mean notifying the shift owner, moving an employee out of an isolated work area, delaying a solo exterior task or using the facility’s controlled entry and safe-area procedure.

Keep the alert on a need-to-know basis. Staff may need the operating instruction without receiving the entire voicemail. “Do not work alone in the rear building until the safety owner clears it” is actionable. Replaying the message to every employee, vendor or customer is not.

The OSHA workplace-violence overview includes threats, harassment and intimidation within workplace violence and notes that employees, customers and visitors can be involved. It also points employers toward worksite-specific prevention plans. A message does not prove that violence will occur, but it is enough to use the plan instead of leaving a single manager to make up a response.

Do not argue through the callback

A callback can expose the employee, alter the record and create new promises. It should occur only when the approved plan assigns a person, purpose and boundary.

The frontline manager should not call back merely to prove courage, demand an apology, test whether the caller is serious or finish the account conversation. They also should not change gate access, remove a lock, reverse a charge, disclose account details or schedule an in-person meeting as an improvised way to calm the caller.

CDC/NIOSH’s basic workplace-violence guidance tells employees to report perceived threats under existing policy and not argue with a customer who makes threats or becomes violent. That publication was developed for a broader workplace context, not as a self-storage callback script. The useful operating principle is restraint: move the event to the authorized process rather than trying to win the exchange.

If a designated person later makes contact, record the authorization, channel, time, participants and outcome as a new event. Do not overwrite the original voicemail record with the callback result.

Give the reviewer a clean evidence packet

The safety owner, supervisor or law-enforcement contact should not have to reconstruct the event from scattered texts. Give them one bounded packet:

  1. facility name, address and exact operating area affected;
  2. discovery time and person who discovered the message;
  3. original system, mailbox and preservation status;
  4. displayed caller information exactly as shown;
  5. exact or qualified working transcript;
  6. known present conditions, including whether the person is observed onsite;
  7. immediate protective actions already taken;
  8. the account or service issue only if relevant and authorized for disclosure;
  9. the decision needed, the named owner and the next review time.

Do not attach an entire customer file by default. Do not add rumors, social-media searches or coworker theories. More information is not automatically better information. The packet should answer what happened, what is known now, what was preserved and who has authority for the next step.

Use states that cannot be confused with closure

“Voicemail saved” does not mean “threat assessed.” “Supervisor notified” does not mean “law enforcement contacted.” “Caller identified” does not mean “person located.” “No one arrived by noon” does not mean “risk resolved.”

Use visible states:

  • Received: the message exists and discovery is recorded.
  • Preserved: the original and metadata are protected.
  • Safety screened: immediate conditions were checked without confrontation.
  • Escalated: the authorized owner received the evidence packet.
  • Authority decision recorded: the owner documented the operating and reporting direction.
  • Disposition pending or closed: follow-up requirements, restrictions and review time are explicit.

Closure requires the owner named by policy, not the passage of time or a quieter front office. The closure record should say what condition changed, which restrictions ended, what evidence supports that decision and whether later review is still required.

A fictional example

The following scenario and every name, time and record are fictional.

At 6:42 a.m., the main voicemail box at Red Willow Storage receives a 38-second message. At 7:03 a.m., the opening manager hears language directed at the office and a statement about arriving later that morning. The displayed number matches a number already visible in the approved account system, but the manager does not treat that match as identity proof or as evidence that the account holder placed the call.

The manager does not call back. From inside the secured office, they check the visible lobby and gate approach, confirm no person or vehicle matching the limited known description is observed, and notify the shift safety owner. The original voicemail remains in the business system. The manager records the message metadata and prepares a working transcript with two uncertain words marked. The safety owner reviews the packet, gives staff a need-to-know operating instruction and selects the applicable law-enforcement contact path. Account resolution remains paused and separate.

At the next review, the record shows the original message is preserved, the immediate-danger screen is complete, the authority decision is documented and the event remains open pending the safety owner’s disposition. Nothing in the fictional record claims the threat was credible, that a crime occurred or that the caller’s identity was established.

The ten-minute opening test

Before the next difficult voicemail arrives, a manager should be able to answer five questions:

  1. Where is the workplace-violence or threat-response procedure?
  2. Who owns the immediate safety decision at opening and after hours?
  3. How is a voicemail preserved without forwarding it to personal devices?
  4. Which number is used for an emergency, and which approved path handles a non-emergency report?
  5. Who can authorize a callback, account change, access restriction and final closure?

If any answer is missing, that is today’s operating work.

Good customer service does not require an employee to absorb a threat alone or improvise a negotiation. A disciplined response protects the people at the facility, protects the integrity of the record and gives the right authority a clean basis for action. That is not overreaction. It is the operating system doing its job before the next phone call changes the facts.

Threatening Voicemail Preservation and Escalation Card

Use this card when a self-storage office receives a voicemail containing a possible threat. It does not decide credibility, criminality or the correct law-enforcement route. Follow the facility’s approved emergency, workplace-violence, privacy and account-access procedures.

1. Screen for immediate danger

  • [ ] Stay in or move to the designated safe area when needed.
  • [ ] Check only conditions visible without confrontation or an unnecessary property search.
  • [ ] Record whether the caller or named person is observed onsite: yes, no or unknown.
  • [ ] Call 911 for immediate physical danger or another emergency requiring police, fire or medical assistance.
  • [ ] Give the call-taker the facility address, callback number, nature of the emergency and observed details.

2. Preserve the original

  • [ ] Do not delete, trim, rename or casually forward the voicemail.
  • [ ] Record the receiving system, mailbox, received time, duration, displayed caller information and message identifier.
  • [ ] Note whether the message was already marked heard when discovered.
  • [ ] Use only the approved export or legal-hold method; do not experiment on the original.
  • [ ] Record the custodian and preservation time.

3. Separate content from interpretation

  • [ ] Prepare a working transcript only when the procedure permits it.
  • [ ] Mark uncertain words, inaudible sections and nonverbal sounds honestly.
  • [ ] Keep exact content, observed conditions and reviewer interpretation in separate fields.
  • [ ] Do not diagnose, insult, minimize or assign intent to the caller.

4. Protect people and control communication

  • [ ] Notify the designated shift or safety owner.
  • [ ] Give staff only the operating instruction they need.
  • [ ] Pause solo exterior work, meetings or access changes when directed.
  • [ ] Do not replay the message publicly or send it to an uncontrolled group.
  • [ ] Do not call back, argue, promise, disclose account details or change access without authority.

5. Hand off one evidence packet

  • [ ] Facility, address and affected operating area
  • [ ] Discovery time and discoverer
  • [ ] Original-message location and preservation status
  • [ ] Displayed caller data exactly as shown
  • [ ] Working transcript with uncertainties marked
  • [ ] Immediate conditions and protective actions
  • [ ] Relevant authorized account context only
  • [ ] Named decision owner, decision needed and next review time

6. Record the decision and closure boundary

Select one current state: received, preserved, safety_screened, escalated, authority_decision_recorded, disposition_pending, or closed.

  • [ ] Record emergency or non-emergency routing separately from supervisor notification.
  • [ ] Record every authorized callback or account action as a new event.
  • [ ] Name each temporary restriction and who can remove it.
  • [ ] Close only when the policy owner records the changed condition, supporting evidence, ended restrictions and any later review.

Prohibited shortcuts

  • Calling back to test whether the caller is serious
  • Deleting the message after making a transcript
  • Treating caller ID or an account match as identity proof
  • Forwarding the voicemail to personal devices or a group chat
  • Labeling the caller unstable, intoxicated, harmless or criminal without evidence
  • Changing gate access, locks, charges or account data to calm the caller
  • Treating elapsed time or non-arrival as closure

This card is an operating aid. The approved facility plan and instructions from emergency services or law enforcement control.
