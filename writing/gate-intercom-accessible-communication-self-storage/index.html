The Customer Can’t Hear the Gate Intercom. What Happens Next?

A shift-ready guide to helping a self-storage customer when the gate intercom is unusable, with an alternate contact method, safe directions and a clear handoff.

By Jared Mastroianni10 min read

In this fictional example, a customer at Alder Reach Storage presses the gate intercom and tells the office they cannot hear the reply. They have a move-in appointment, and there is a vehicle waiting behind them. The manager can hear the customer clearly. From the office, the intercom seems to be doing its job.

The customer still has no way to get the next instruction. If the only alternative is a phone number that leads to the same voice exchange, the facility has sent them in a circle. The gate equipment may be working. The service process has stalled.

Treat the first exchange as two jobs. Help the customer reach a safe place to communicate and find a method that works for them. Then complete the normal steps for the visit through that method. You can change the way you talk with someone and still protect the account, the gate and everyone in the lane.

If traffic is building, use the site’s approved traffic procedure. Give directions only for a route staff can confirm is open and suitable for the vehicle. If the customer has no way to receive them yet, send a staff member only if they can make contact from a protected location under the site procedure. An unread message or a gesture from the office is too little to rely on around a moving gate.

Make the next instruction easy to find

Walk the entrance from the customer’s point of view. Where can a driver first see the help instructions? Can the driver read them before reaching the point where another vehicle can queue behind them? Is there an approved place to stop away from gate travel and moving vehicles? If you publish directions for that location, use an actual landmark people can recognize from the approach.

Post the options the site currently supports. A monitored text number may work at one store. Another may have a staffed office reachable by a suitable route, a supported two-way help system or a phone team that handles relay calls. Say which options are monitored during which hours. Place the same information where customers prepare for a visit, especially in move-in instructions and the facility’s access-help page. A QR code can supplement the directions. Keep the essential contact information in readable text too.

The time to test a sign is before it goes up. Use a staff drill from the approach, without exposing anyone to gate movement or blocking traffic. Follow the wording exactly. If the sign leads to an unattended mailbox, a confusing entrance or a response that asks the customer to call the intercom again, the route still needs work.

Ask what works for this customer

When you make contact, ask what would make the exchange work now. A customer who is deaf or hard of hearing may use written communication, a relay service, an interpreter or another method. An exchange about where to meet may be straightforward. A disputed balance, a lease discussion or a complex change in access can require more support.

The U.S. Department of Justice explains that covered entities must provide assistance when needed for effective communication. The appropriate method depends on the person's usual way of communicating and on the nature, length, complexity and setting of the exchange. For covered public accommodations, the Title III rule also addresses timely, accessible communication that protects privacy and independence. Use those principles to prepare your response. Questions about how the rules apply to particular premises or obligations belong with the operator’s accessibility lead or qualified adviser. DOJ guidance on effective communication · DOJ Title III regulations, § 36.303

For a simple direction, a written note or approved business messaging channel may be effective if it works for the customer. Speak or write to the customer directly. If someone accompanying them offers to take over, confirm what the customer wants and follow the site’s procedure for communication assistance. Staff should be ready to arrange help without requiring customers to bring someone to interpret for them.

If the first alternative is ineffective, keep ownership of the request. Contact the person who can arrange the appropriate support. Confirm a workable way to send an update and when the customer can expect it. A referral into another voice-only queue just moves the problem.

Use the accessible channel to complete the same job

Once contact is working, identify what the customer came to do. Meeting a manager for a scheduled move-in, entering on an active rental and asking about a blocked account require different decisions. Check the existing visit or account record through the normal authorized workflow. Where the standard method relies on speaking or hearing, use the equivalent approved verification path or ask the duty manager to arrange one. Keep the request open while that manager works on it.

The first message can carry a location, a safe next instruction and who will help. Move account information to the approved private channel. Avoid asking a customer to display a credential in the lane or share an access code on a public help line. If the site needs identification, use its normal protected process and give the customer an accessible way to complete it.

Two short confirmations help. First, establish that the customer received and understood the directions. Then confirm the next service step happened or name the person who will finish it. A message marked delivered is useful evidence about the channel. It cannot tell you whether the customer found the meeting point or completed the visit.

Take relay calls through the normal queue

A relay call can begin with a communications assistant explaining the service. Give the exchange time. Address the customer, use the normal verification safeguards in an accessible form, and keep responsibility with the person who answers. The FCC’s consumer guide explains how communications assistants connect callers and points out that 711 reaches certain forms of relay. A customer may already use another form of relay that connects to the facility’s telephone number. FCC consumer guide to telecommunications relay service

For a covered public accommodation, the DOJ’s Title III rule requires it to respond to Title IV relay calls as it responds to other calls. Train the front desk and any answering service to recognize those calls and complete the normal handoff. Test the entire route, including menus, vendor queues and transfers, through an authorized exercise. The advertised number is only as useful as the response a caller can actually reach.

Leave a record the next shift can use

Write down where the exchange stopped and what the team did to restore it. A short entry can record the gate or lane, time, channel requested, channel used, staff owner, visit status and any promised follow-up. Record the obstacle in operational language. “Voice intercom unusable for this exchange; customer confirmed directions by business text” tells the next manager what worked. A diagnostic label or medical detail adds little to that record.

Back at fictional Alder Reach, the move-in instructions had identified a waiting area reachable from the approach and listed the monitored business text number. Once stopped in that area, the customer uses the number to confirm their location. The manager sends directions to the staffed office and confirms the customer can follow them. Staff complete the move-in verification privately at the counter using the agreed communication method and assist with entry through the normal gate process. The shift note also records that the posted alternate number was hard to spot from the approach. A named employee owns that correction.

Keep equipment checks and communication fixes on their own tracks. If multiple customers report clipped audio, open a maintenance item with a test location and the times reported. If someone can hear the intercom and still cannot use the voice exchange, the team should check whether the alternative was easy to find, promptly answered and effective. Those are different clues about what the operation needs to fix.

Practice it during the next opening walk

Use the Gate Help and Communication Shift Card with a manager and a second employee. Run it from the approach, the office and the after-hours route. Start with the fiction that the voice exchange is unavailable. The manager should be able to point to the next visible direction, identify who can answer, describe how to verify a customer’s authority privately and record the final service state.

Have the second employee play a customer with no usable mobile data. See whether the backup depends on a smartphone or a staff member who has already gone home. Try a different situation in which a short written exchange is insufficient. The drill passes when staff can move the case to someone who can arrange help, give a meaningful next update and maintain ownership.

I would treat a failed drill as a customer-service repair. Fix the sign, shift coverage, routing or procedure at the point where the exchange broke. Then test the corrected step from the same spot. A manager should be able to show the next person on shift how the customer gets all the way from the gate to the help they came for.

Gate Help Communication Shift Card

Use this card from the gate approach, the office and the after-hours support desk. Fill in the site’s actual routes and contacts before a drill.

Prepare the route

Confirm Record
Visible direction Where the customer first sees an alternate contact method and the way to a safe waiting location
Live contact Approved communication channels, staffed hours and who answers each one
After-hours help Published route, who owns it and who can arrange additional support
Private verification Accessible verification method and the duty manager who can approve an equivalent path
Service finish How the team will confirm the customer received directions and completed the next service step

Work the request

  • ☐ Give a safe next direction and use a communication method that works for the customer.
  • ☐ Ask what help is needed. Confirm the request and next step directly with the customer.
  • ☐ Move account questions and verification to an approved private channel.
  • ☐ Confirm the service step or assign a person and time for the next update.

Test the route

Drill condition Passing evidence
Voice intercom unusable Alternate route is visible and reaches a responder without requiring the same voice exchange.
No usable mobile data The duty manager can identify a usable planned route or arrange an effective alternative with a named owner.
Written directions insufficient Staff can arrange more suitable assistance and give the customer a usable update.
Relay call The call is handled through the normal service queue and its verification steps, including any transfer.
