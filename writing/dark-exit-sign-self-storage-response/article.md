# The Exit Sign Is Dark. Treat the Route as Unverified Until the Marking Is Restored

**Deck:** An unlit exit sign is not merely a bulb ticket. It changes what the manager can truthfully say about route visibility, so the response needs an operating boundary, an escalation owner and evidence before release.

**By Jared Mastroianni**

The opening walk is almost finished when the manager notices that the red letters over a corridor door are dark. The word “Exit” is still visible in the morning light, and customers are due to arrive in ten minutes.

That is the moment to resist the easy label: **burned-out bulb**.

A dark exit sign is an observation. It is not a diagnosis, and it is not a complete statement about the route. The manager does not yet know whether the sign lost normal power, its backup failed, a circuit is impaired, the face is damaged or another condition exists. More important, the manager cannot establish from appearance alone whether the required marking and related safeguards are ready for the conditions the facility may face.

The practical response is to control the affected operating area, follow the site emergency plan, escalate to the designated safety owner and qualified service provider, and keep the route in an explicit unverified state until the authorized release evidence exists.

## Start with what you can actually see

Record the facts before anyone resets a breaker, moves a sign or starts guessing.

Identify the exact device and location. Note the time, the observer, the sign-face condition, ambient lighting and whether the line of sight to the sign is clear. Record what prompted the observation: an opening walk, a customer report, an alarm inspection or another source. Photograph the condition if policy allows, without delaying an urgent safety response.

Use plain statements:

- “Exit sign ES-2 above the east corridor discharge door is not illuminated.”
- “The word Exit is visible under current ambient lighting.”
- “Cause and backup-power state are unknown.”

Do not write “bulb failed,” “battery dead,” “circuit good” or “safe to use” unless an authorized person has established that fact through an approved method. A maintenance shorthand can become a false technical conclusion when it travels into a ticket, shift note or regional report.

## Treat marking as part of the route

The federal workplace rule is direct about the relationship between a route and its marking. Under 29 CFR 1910.37, employee exit routes must be adequately lighted, each exit must be clearly visible and marked by an Exit sign, required directional signs must be posted when travel is not immediately apparent, and the line of sight to an exit sign must remain visible. The same section requires emergency safeguards, including exit lighting, to remain in proper working order.

That rule applies to workplaces and employee exit routes. It is not a full building-code, fire-code, accessibility or public-occupancy determination for a particular self-storage facility. Local requirements and the approved site plan may be more specific. But the operating lesson is sound: the sign is not decoration next to the route. Its condition is part of the route state.

That means the manager should not close a ticket because the door still opens or daylight makes the letters readable at 8 a.m. The facility needs an answer that remains valid for the authorized operating condition, not only the instant in which the problem was discovered.

## Put the area in a named operating state

Avoid the vague phrase “monitoring.” Assign one of three states:

**Normal:** The required route, marking and safeguards have the current release evidence defined by the site.

**Controlled exception:** The impairment is documented, the approved emergency or alternate-route procedure is active, access boundaries and communications are in place, and a named owner is driving the response.

**Unavailable:** The facility cannot support normal use of the affected area under the approved plan. Access stays restricted until the designated authority changes the state.

The frontline manager should not invent which state applies. The emergency plan, fire/life-safety procedure and designated authority should define it. If the manager cannot reach the required authority, the state does not default to normal.

For some sites, an approved alternate route may allow limited operation. For others, the impaired sign may affect an area that must be closed. The right answer depends on the facility design, occupancy, approved plans, local requirements and the actual impairment. A generic article cannot make that determination. A good operating system makes the local decision path easy to find.

## Control first, troubleshoot second

When the area is not in normal state, place only the controls the site has approved. Those may include restricting customer entry, stationing a trained employee, activating approved temporary wayfinding, using a verified alternate route, changing access hours or closing an affected building.

Temporary controls must not create their own hazard. Do not block another exit, obscure a sign, narrow the route with cones, tape a fire door open, or direct people through an unverified path. Do not assume a handwritten arrow is an acceptable substitute for required marking. The designated authority needs to confirm the control and its boundaries.

Then create the service request. Give the provider the asset ID, exact location, observed condition, time discovered, current area state and site contact. Ask for the visit and required test evidence; do not prescribe the repair unless that is within your role.

Frontline staff should not open an electrical fixture, work on energized equipment, climb to inspect wiring or perform an improvised power-loss test. A dark sign does not authorize a maintenance task outside training, policy or qualification.

## Keep the emergency plan connected to the incident

Where an OSHA standard requires an emergency action plan, 29 CFR 1910.38 calls for procedures to report an emergency, procedures for evacuation and exit-route assignments, and a named person or role employees can contact for more information. The plan must also be reviewed when employee responsibilities change.

The manager does not need to turn every impaired sign into an evacuation drill. The manager does need to know which plan governs, whom to call and what changes for staff while the exception is open.

Brief the next shift with the same facts and state. Say which area is affected, what customers may do, what staff must not do, who owns the service ticket and when the next update is due. If conditions change, update the record rather than relying on a text thread or a note taped behind the counter.

## Release the route on evidence, not optimism

“The light is back on” is useful information, but it may not be the whole release requirement. The site should define who can restore normal status and what evidence that person needs.

The closeout might require the qualified provider’s repair description, an approved functional test, confirmation of normal and emergency operation, a photo, a service-ticket number and a final route walk. The exact requirement belongs to the applicable authority and site procedure. The manager’s job is to preserve the chain: observed condition, interim state, service action, test evidence, release authority and release time.

Do not backdate the release to when the provider arrived. Do not close the route exception while a related ticket remains unresolved unless the authorized owner explicitly separates the issues. If the sign is illuminated but the route remains obstructed, the sign repair does not make the route normal.

## A fictional example

**The following scenario and every name, identifier, time, condition and outcome in it are fictional. They exist only to demonstrate the method.**

At fictional Cedar Ferry Storage, the opening manager sees that exit sign ES-2 above the east corridor discharge door is dark. She records the observation at 7:42 a.m. and marks the cause, emergency-power state and code determination unknown. She does not call it a dead battery.

The site procedure directs her to the district safety lead. That lead places the east corridor in controlled-exception status, confirms the approved west route for the limited area involved and authorizes the facility’s existing customer-access boundary. The manager briefs the arriving employee and opens service ticket CF-4821 with the approved electrical contractor.

At 9:18 a.m., the fictional contractor records a component replacement and completes the test required by the fictional site procedure. The manager then performs the assigned route walk and uploads the evidence references. The district safety lead releases the east corridor to normal at 9:31 a.m.

The record does not claim that the facility was legally compliant, that every occupant was safe, or that the repair prevented an incident. It shows only what was observed, which operating state was authorized, what work was reported, what evidence was attached and who released the route.

## Use the control card on the next walk

The accompanying **Exit-Sign and Route-Visibility Control Card** is designed for the moment the condition is discovered. It keeps device facts, route state, interim controls, service evidence and release authority in one record.

The most important fields are the ones teams often skip: technical diagnosis state, power state known, alternate route verified, current operating state, next update time, test authority and release evidence.

If one of those answers is missing, leave it unknown. Do not turn a blank into “normal.”

An unlit exit sign may ultimately require a small repair. The operating decision is larger. A manager must know what can continue, what must stop, who owns the technical work and what proves the affected route can return to normal.

That is how a ten-minute opening-walk discovery becomes a controlled exception instead of an undocumented assumption.
