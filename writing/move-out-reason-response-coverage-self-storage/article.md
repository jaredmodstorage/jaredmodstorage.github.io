# Before You Rank Sites by Move-Out Reasons, Count Who Answered

By Jared Mastroianni

![Jared Mastroianni speaks with a facility employee and customer in an AI-generated editorial front-counter scene.](assets/033-facility-front-counter-customer-conversation.png)

*The front counter captures one kind of move-out feedback. A portfolio review also needs to account for customers who close out remotely. AI-generated editorial illustration.*

<!-- BODY_START -->

At two fictional self-storage facilities, the regional manager opens the monthly move-out report. At Juniper Slip, 20 customers picked price as their main reason for leaving. At Slate Landing, 10 did. Both facilities had 100 eligible move-outs, yet Slate Landing’s dashboard shows 50 percent leaving because of price and Juniper Slip’s shows 40 percent.

The dashboard is dividing by the people who answered the question. At Juniper Slip, 50 customers answered. At Slate Landing, 20 did. Slate Landing shows a higher share among respondents, while Juniper Slip has twice as many documented price responses. The report gives a useful lead for investigation. Ordering stores by why all customers left would require stronger evidence.

I want a portfolio review to show how a reason got into the report. Start with the move-out register. Reconcile invitations and usable answers back to it. Then show which parts of the departing customer population the answers actually describe. At a facility about to change prices or staffing, those steps are worth more than another decimal place.

## Start with completed move-outs and a stable unit of count

Define the population before you open the survey export. For a voluntary departure review, start with completed rental endings in the reporting window and apply the agreed eligibility rule. Decide whether internal transfers or operator-initiated terminations belong in a separate cohort. Remove voided or reversed events under a documented rule. An eligible customer with no permitted contact channel still belongs in the eligible count. Their invitation status explains why the team could not ask.

Be precise about what a row represents. A lease ending is a workable unit if the business wants to explain departing rentals. A business customer ending three leases is three rental endings. That is one customer if the question is why customers left altogether. Choose a measure and use it across the portfolio. If one questionnaire covers several leases, document how it maps to the reporting unit and avoid assigning the same answer to three rentals unless the question and record support that scope.

Tie the case to the facility and effective departure date. Retain the invitation and response timestamps as separate fields. That lets a team compare November departures fairly even if some answers arrive in December. Give each cohort the same response window measured from the move-out event, or mark newer cohorts provisional until the window closes. State whether you leave a closed report fixed or issue a dated revision for late answers.

## Reconcile the path from eligible to answered

For each site and move-out channel, show eligible cases, unique invitations sent and usable answers to the reason question. Start with a current status per case. A customer might receive an invitation, an allowed reminder and an in-person prompt. Those contacts still represent one case. Preserve attempted contacts as a separate history so you can tell why a case was missed without counting it twice.

Use mutually exclusive outcomes at the reporting cutoff. Count each case once as never invited, delivery failed without a usable answer, invited with no reply, declined or skipped, or answered. If someone responds after a failed attempt through a second channel, their final outcome is answered and the failure stays in the history. Keep the invitation flag as its own field. That way the status counts still add to the eligible total, and a provider error can be investigated without erasing the customer’s answer.

Use three explicit denominators. Invitation coverage is invitations sent divided by eligible move-outs. Answer coverage is usable answers divided by eligible move-outs. The reported share for a reason is selections for that reason divided by usable answers, under a one-primary-reason rule. You can also report answers divided by invitations as a diagnostic of the invitation path when every counted answer can be tied to a counted invitation. Label the metric so it stays distinct from coverage of the eligible population.

The American Association for Public Opinion Research distinguishes case dispositions and survey outcome rates, and explains that the rate alone cannot establish the presence or size of nonresponse error. The same discipline helps here. These are internal coverage measures for a defined group of rental endings. A response rate from a formal survey design answers a more specific methodological question. [AAPOR Standard Definitions](https://aapor.org/standards-and-ethics/standard-definitions/)

## Keep the question and the evidence source comparable

Use the same neutral prompt for the comparison you want. Asking for the main reason produces a different result from asking customers to select every reason that applies. If you collect several reasons, report the share selecting each one and expect the shares to sum to more than 100 percent when people choose multiple reasons. Do not silently convert the first selection into the main reason.

Keep the option set and ordering with the question version. A site that combines price, fees and value will not yield a clean comparison with one that offers them separately. Preserve the original response and apply a documented mapping only where the meaning holds. If a new version adds an access or condition option, mark the transition in the report. Repeat the question on comparable channels before interpreting a shift as a change in what customers value. Pew Research Center’s methodology guide explains why wording, format and question order can change responses. [Pew Research Center, Writing Survey Questions](https://www.pewresearch.org/writing-survey-questions/)

Record who supplied the reason. A customer selecting price in a portal and a manager selecting price after looking at the account are different evidence. A staff member can transcribe a reason given directly by the customer if the record shows how it was obtained. Keep staff inferences in their own field. An account history may show a rate change, but it cannot supply the person’s reason for leaving. Preserve “other,” “prefer not to answer” and “no answer” as separate states. Keep private comments out of portfolio reports unless their use is approved and necessary.

## Check which departures are missing before ranking stores

Return to the fictional figures. Juniper Slip sent 80 invitations and captured 50 usable answers. Slate Landing sent 40 and captured 20. Invitation coverage is 80 versus 40 percent. Answer coverage is 50 versus 20 percent. Among people who supplied a primary reason, price is 20 of 50 versus 10 of 20. Write the result in those terms and the reader can see both the comparison and its limitation.

Now split the same fictional 100 departures per site by move-out route. Each store has 50 counter closeouts and 50 remote closeouts. Juniper Slip obtained 35 counter answers and 15 remote answers. Slate Landing obtained 18 counter answers and two remote answers. That puts Slate Landing’s remote answer coverage at four percent. The site manager has a concrete question to investigate. Are remote move-outs entering the invitation process consistently, and is the method suitable for that route?

The coverage gap leaves the direction of any bias unknown. Each store’s nonrespondents could have different motives than its respondents, and even matched answer coverage would leave that possibility open. The Federal Committee on Statistical Methodology recommends examining respondent and nonrespondent differences where usable frame data exist. For a site review, compare a small set of operational fields already recorded for the whole group, such as move-out route, tenancy band or unit class. Those comparisons help identify whose answers are missing. They cannot reconstruct private motives. [Federal Committee on Statistical Methodology, Best Practices for Nonresponse Bias Reporting](https://statspolicy.gov/assets/fcsm/files/docs/FCSM%20NRBA%20Report%20062623.pdf)

Trace a few missed remote cases from the completed departure event to the invitation log before changing the survey. You might find that one route closes the lease without creating a survey task, or that a valid contact restriction prevents an invitation. The workflow owner can fix the first issue and count the second accurately. Keep the question optional. A customer should be able to finish a move-out without giving a reason, and staff should have a way to close the record without selecting one on the customer’s behalf.

If someone insists the Slate Landing figure is a population result, show the limits using the fictional counts. Ten of 100 eligible move-outs had a documented price answer. If every one of the 80 missing answers would have picked price, the figure would be 90 of 100. Juniper Slip’s corresponding range is 20 to 70 of 100. These are extreme-case bounds for the share that could select price under the stated question and a single primary response. They are useful for showing how much the missing answers matter. They are neither an estimate nor a confidence interval.

## Match the decision to the strength of the evidence

Put one of three labels on the portfolio row. **Investigation only** is appropriate while the cohort, response count or question versions differ materially. **Respondent comparison** is appropriate when the definitions align and the report shows counts, coverage and material differences in who answered. **Population estimate** belongs in a separate workflow with a defensible design and analysis. Avoid promoting a dashboard using a universal answer-coverage cutoff. Even a high count can come from a systematically different group.

Make the next action explicit. A break in invitations goes to whoever owns the move-out workflow. A question change goes to whoever owns portfolio reporting. If a price theme repeats in comparable answers, the regional lead can review the actual customer experience, including rate notices, published fees, payment or service friction, and relevant account history. Make a price change only after the operating decision has its own evidence and authority. Use reported reasons to find what to examine and take genuine complaints seriously while the review is still open.

Use the Move-Out Reason Coverage Worksheet on two stores this week. Pull completed move-outs, reconcile case status, split counter and remote results, and write one sentence the counts actually support. Assign one person to the biggest gap and agree on what evidence will close it. At the next review, check whether the portion of the cohort you hear from has changed before interpreting a change in the answers.
