The Customer Asked to Borrow the Ladder. The Answer Needs a Procedure, Not a Favor

A facility ladder is not a courtesy item. Before it changes hands, the manager needs a written use boundary, an identified asset, a condition decision, a responsible custodian and a documented closeout.

By Jared Mastroianni9 min read

Research synthesis, drafting and quality assurance were AI-assisted under Jared Mastroianni’s byline. Fictional examples are instructional, not observed results.

A customer is standing at the counter and asks, “Can I borrow your ladder for five minutes?” The request sounds ordinary. The unit is nearby. The ladder is visible in the maintenance area. Saying yes may feel like helpful service.

That is exactly why the moment needs a procedure.

The manager does not yet know what the customer plans to reach, where the ladder will stand, whether the selected ladder is suitable, whether it was inspected, or whether customer use is allowed at all. A quick favor can quietly transfer facility equipment into an uncontrolled task. Once that happens, the team may lose track of the asset, the condition of the work area and who is responsible for stopping unsafe use.

The right first response is neither a reflexive yes nor an argumentative no. It is a controlled pause: “Let me check our equipment policy and the exact item before anything is released.”

Start with the use boundary

Every facility should be able to answer one basic question in writing: Is this ladder staff equipment, customer equipment or an asset approved for both under defined conditions?

If the policy says staff use only, the decision is complete. The ladder does not leave staff custody. Do not create a one-time exception because the customer seems experienced, the task appears brief or another employee remembers allowing it before. Those details do not replace an approved use boundary.

If the policy is silent, treat the request as not authorized pending review. Silence is not permission. Record the request, contact the designated supervisor or safety owner and give the customer an approved alternative. The manager should not write a new lending policy while a customer waits at the counter.

If customer use is expressly allowed, that does not mean the ladder can simply be handed over. It means the manager can proceed to the rest of the release check.

Separate customer service from equipment control

Good service is not measured by how quickly an employee says yes. It is measured by whether the customer receives a clear answer, a useful next step and consistent treatment.

A manager can be helpful without taking ownership of the customer’s task. Explain the site rule in plain language. Offer only alternatives the company has approved. Those might include allowing the customer to return with suitable equipment, rescheduling the visit, or directing the customer to a general public information source. Do not select a ladder for the customer, enter the unit, rearrange stored contents, climb on the customer’s behalf or recommend a specific outside provider unless those actions are expressly within policy.

This distinction matters because “we do not lend facility ladders” and “let me do the task for you” can create two different kinds of uncontrolled work. The first hands over an asset. The second changes the employee’s assignment and may expose the employee to a fall hazard or an unfamiliar condition inside a customer-controlled space.

Identify the exact asset before discussing release

“The ladder” is not a sufficient record. The manager needs an asset number or another unique identifier tied to the physical item.

That identifier should connect to the manufacturer label, rated capacity, intended configuration, inspection history and any open defect or repair record. A ladder that cannot be matched to its record should not be released. A ladder with a missing or unreadable label should be handled according to the site’s defect-control procedure, not evaluated from memory.

For employee use, federal general-industry rules require employers to ensure that ladders are used for their designed purpose, inspected before initial use during each work shift and removed from service when defective. The rules also address stable surfaces, intended load and traffic exposure. Those requirements are useful design inputs for a facility’s internal control, but they govern employers and employees; they do not create a universal customer-lending policy for self-storage operators. The site still needs its own approved answer for customer use.

Use five release gates

When policy allows the request to be evaluated, use the same five gates every time.

1. Authorization

Confirm the current written policy, the allowed user class and who has decision authority. Record the exact policy reference. “We usually allow it” is not a control.

2. Asset condition

Match the physical ladder to the asset record. Confirm the current inspection state, readable labels and absence of an open defect or tag-out. If condition is unknown, the result is do not release until an authorized inspection resolves it. A missing answer is not a pass.

3. Designed use and environment

Record the proposed task and location. Compare them with the equipment’s designed use and the site’s approved conditions. Look for unstable or slippery surfaces, doorways, drive lanes, energized equipment, overhead hazards and other activity that could displace the ladder. Do not improvise a configuration or combine equipment to gain height.

The Occupational Safety and Health Administration’s portable-ladder guidance reinforces basic controls such as following labels, inspecting before use, using equipment only for its designed purpose, keeping it on a stable surface and respecting load ratings. That guidance is written for occupational safety. It can inform the facility’s procedure, but it does not make an employee qualified to approve customer use outside company policy.

4. Custody

Name the person responsible for the asset from release through return. Define where it may be used, when it is due back and what happens if the task or location changes. A ladder should not circulate through the property on an informal “bring it back when you’re done” understanding.

5. Closeout

Record the return time and condition before placing the ladder back in service. If the ladder was dropped, struck, contaminated, used in an unapproved location or returned with uncertain condition, quarantine it for the required inspection. Close the request only after the asset and any follow-up action have an owner.

Keep employee use in its own lane

A manager may decide that customer use is prohibited but employee use is possible. That is not an automatic workaround.

The employee’s task must be authorized, the work area must be assessed and applicable training must be current. The federal general-industry training rule requires employers to train employees in the proper care, inspection, storage and use of covered equipment before they use it, and to retrain when circumstances show that knowledge or skill is inadequate. A counter employee should not become a ladder user simply because the customer is waiting.

If the employee task is outside the employee’s role, involves customer contents, requires entering a customer unit or presents a hazard the site has not controlled, stop and escalate. The customer request does not redefine the employee’s job.

A fictional example

The following scenario is fictional and is included only to demonstrate the procedure.

At fictional Alder Row Storage, a customer asks to borrow a six-foot stepladder to reach a box stacked near the rear of a unit. The manager opens the equipment-request card before touching the ladder. The site policy identifies the ladder as staff-use-only maintenance equipment. Customer use is not authorized.

The manager records the request as not released — user class not authorized. She explains the decision without debating the customer’s experience: “Our facility ladder is assigned to trained staff work and cannot be loaned to customers. You can return with equipment appropriate for your task.” She does not enter the unit, move the box or offer to climb for the customer.

Later, the maintenance lead confirms that the ladder remains in its designated storage location. No condition claim is added because the ladder was never evaluated or used for this request. The record closes with the policy reference, decision, customer message and asset-custody confirmation.

The result is not dramatic. That is the point. The facility gave a consistent answer, retained control of its equipment and did not turn an ordinary request into undocumented work.

Put the card where the question happens

The accompanying Customer Equipment Request and Custody Card is designed for the counter, not a monthly safety meeting. It captures the request, policy boundary, exact asset, condition state, proposed environment, decision, customer message, custody and return.

Use three explicit decision states:

  • Released: Every required gate passed and the named decision owner approved the release.
  • Not released: A policy, user, condition, task or environment gate failed.
  • Pending review: Required information or authority is missing. Nothing changes hands.

That last state prevents a common mistake: treating incomplete information as low risk. “I do not know” is not permission. It is a stop until the right owner resolves the question.

Make consistency visible

Once the procedure exists, review the records for patterns. Repeated requests may show that customers misunderstand what equipment the facility provides. Frequent missing asset IDs may show that tools are not labeled. Repeated pending decisions may show that the escalation path is too slow or the policy is unclear.

Those trends are useful only if the record stays factual. Do not convert a customer’s stated experience into verified qualification. Do not treat a completed form as proof that the task was safe. Do not infer a successful outcome merely because no incident was reported. Record what was observed, what policy authorized and what action the facility took.

A ladder request should never depend on which manager is at the desk or how busy the property feels. The answer should come from the same controlled sequence every time: written boundary, exact asset, condition, environment, custody and closeout.

That is not unfriendly. It is dependable service.
